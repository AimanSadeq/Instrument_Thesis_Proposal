Abstract
This literature review synthesizes forty studies from leading finance journals to examine the role of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) in modern finance. While traditional econometric models such as CAPM and Fama-MacBeth regressions remain influential, ML methods, including tree-based algorithms, neural networks, and natural language processing, deliver stronger predictive performance by capturing nonlinearities and high-dimensional interactions. Applications span asset pricing, credit markets, financial reporting, climate risk, and robo-advisory, where AI demonstrates tangible benefits in forecasting and operations. However, challenges persist: many models lose effectiveness once transaction costs and frictions are considered, interpretability and fairness remain critical concerns, and external validity across markets is often limited. Governance issues, particularly those related to transparency and accountability in regulated contexts, further complicate the adoption process. The review concludes that AI and ML complement rather than replace finance theory, offering valuable tools when combined with rigorous validation, explainable frameworks, and sensitivity to institutional and regulatory constraints.

Introduction
The rapid development of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) has transformed the empirical toolkit of finance, and increasingly, the questions we ask of markets, institutions, and investors. This literature review synthesizes approximately forty core studies from leading finance journals (Complemented by a broader scan of 145 abstracts drawn from the Journal of Financial Data Science (JFDS) to assess what ML adds beyond traditional econometrics, when it adds it, and at what cost to interpretability, implementability, and fairness. It proceeds from foundations (Section 2) to historical evolution (Section 3), domain applications (Section 4), governance and ethics (Section 5), methodological benchmarking (Section 6), and open problems and research directions (Section 7), concluding with a summary (Section 8). Together, these sections argue that ML’s genuine gains, flexible function approximation, richer use of text and alternative data, and robustness in high-dimensional settings are real but contingent upon rigorous validation and institutional constraints.
Section 2 anchors the review in finance theory and decision frameworks. It contrasts the expectations of efficient market hypothesis (EMH), capital assets pricing model (CAPM), and arbitrage portfolio theory (APT) with evidence that complex models can uncover persistent nonlinear structure in returns and risk, reframing the prediction‑versus‑inference debate and positioning “AI systems” within decision theory and explainable AI (XAI). It also introduces interdisciplinary lenses, behavioral finance, economics, social & governance (ESG) integration, and technology acceptance, which recur throughout the review’s applications.
Section 3 traces the field’s transition: from linear, low‑dimensional econometrics to ML, then to deep learning and sequence/image models, alongside a parallel rise of XAI and the emergence of robo‑advisory. The section emphasizes that method choice evolved not only with advances in computing power and data scale, but also with the empirical recognition that financial relations are often nonlinear and interactive in nature.
Section 4 organizes empirical domains where ML has been most influential: asset pricing and return predictability; text, disclosure, and financial reporting; and sustainability/climate. It documents ML’s gains in extracting priced information from macro/market states and narrative text, as well as in quantifying climate/ESG exposures that are material for valuation and risk.
Section 5 turns to governance. It highlights three important requirements: explainability, fairness, and accountability. It also examines two main risks: algorithmic bias and systems working together in harmful ways. The section outlines the practical guardrails (audits, disclosures, and model documentation) required when ML systems impact access to credit, pricing, or market conduct.
Section 6 benchmarks methods and evaluation practice. It inventories model families used across the corpus and underscores that out-of-sample, cost-aware validation, not in-sample fit, underwrites credible claims to “edge.” The section also highlights rising attention to interpretability and stakeholder impact in model comparisons.
Section 7 consolidates open problems into a research agenda. Key challenges include translating statistical power into implementable strategies under frictions, balancing complexity with interpretable economic structure, establishing external validity across markets and microstructure regimes, and designing effective man‑plus‑machine workflows.
Taken together, Sections 2–7 position ML as a complement, not a replacement, to economic reasoning. The promise is strongest where flexible models reveal economically interpretable signals that persist out-of-sample and after costs; the pitfalls arise when impenetrability, portability, and governance are neglected. The remainder of the review elaborates on these claims in detail.

The role of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) in financial research requires more than just technical knowledge; it requires a clear understanding of theoretical frameworks such as the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), and the Black-Scholes Model. These theories not only form how models are constructed but also affect our expectations of their capabilities and limits. [Heading2] 2.1 Traditional Financial Theories
Classical financial theories have traditionally provided scholars and practitioners with ways to measure risk, return, and market dynamics. I will present three examples by comparing what the theory states and how research done using ML models shows different results from the theory. 
2.11 - Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)
One important theory is the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH), which states that asset prices fully reflect all available information, thereby negating the possibility that investors can outperform the market. That said, the theory implies that historical data is of limited predictive power and, as such, will not be beneficial to analyze so that an investor can predict an investment decision. However, (Murray et al., 2024)challenge this view by showing that non-linear ML models can detect pricing inefficiencies, suggesting that market behavior may not be as random as the EMH claims. This is particularly more evident with high-frequency and alternative datasets. This is an important finding that proposes that ML models can be used to validate or evaluate existing finance theories for viability and consistency.
2.12 - Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)
The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) theory proposes a linear relationship between systematic risk and expected return. Although widely popular and used in Academia teaching and investment practices, it has struggled to explain empirical dissimilarities such as size, value, and momentum results. To that effect, (Drobetz et al., 2024) use ML techniques to extend CAPM's architecture, incorporating how the impact of one variable on the outcome may depend on another variable (interaction terms) and non-linear variables. This adaptive approach offers a more flexible representation of risk-return trade-offs.
2.13 - Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT)
The Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT) states that an asset's returns can be forecasted with the linear relationship of an asset's expected returns and the macroeconomic factors that affect the asset's risk. In principle, this theory aligns well with the logic of ML models that seek hidden structures within high-dimensional data. (Chen et al., 2024) align with APT's emphasis on the relationship between asset returns and their exposure to systematic risk factors.

[Heading2] 2.2 AI and Decision Theory
AI-based Systems (when AI is a component among other technologies such as data platforms, decision engines, and trading frameworks) inherently operate within environments of uncertainty and incomplete information, placing them within the domain of decision theory. To that, a classical decision theory focuses on rational investors making optimal choices under probabilistic expectations. In the same wavelength, AI systems, which are the core AI model or algorithm itself, such as neural networks or reinforcement learning agents, adapt by learning from experience and evolving their decision policies over time.
“AI-based systems”: introduces the environment/context vs.
“AI systems”: narrows in on the models themselves.
Yet this adaptability comes at a cost, particularly in interpretability. Meaning that high-performing models like deep neural networks often function as "black boxes", a popular term used in AI LLM Models, producing outputs that may lack a clear explanation. The growing field of Explainable AI (XAI) addresses this challenge by developing tools that enhance transparency and traceability. The important work done by (De Bock et al., 2023) underscores the importance of these tools, especially in highly regulated financial settings where accountability is non-negotiable.
In addition, AI introduces another dilemma, a normative one: 
1 - Can decision-makers trust models they do not fully understand?
2 - What should count as sufficient evidence to rely on in a model’s decision?
3 - Who ought to be held responsible for AI-driven outcomes? 
These questions matter most in areas like credit scoring, algorithmic trading, or compliance, where decisions influenced by cloudy models may breed systemic risk.
[Heading2] 2.3 Machine Learning Principles in Predictive Analytics
Machine learning has reshaped financial modeling by emphasizing prediction over inference. While inference tries to explain relationships between variables and test hypothesis, ask a question of "Does X cause Y or how much does X affect Y?", with an output in coefficients, confidence intervals, and p-values and, and has a high level of interpretability of results, prediction is concerned with generating accurate forecasts for new or unseen data, ask a question of "what is Y, given X?", with an output of accurate predictions, even if the model’s internal logic is not fully interpretable (i.e. black box). That said, ML prioritizes accuracy in forecasting future outcomes, even if that means sacrificing interpretability. Dare old school is inference, and new school is prediction? Remember, both have an area of inconclusively, at a varying level of confidence. 
Supervised learning techniques involve training models on labelled datasets, where the correct outcomes are known, to predict future values accurately, using algorithms such as decision trees, support vector machines, ensemble models, and deep neural networks. These models are later optimized through the application of advanced techniques such as cross-validation, regularization, and hyperparameter tuning. (Chen et al., 2024) demonstrate that deep learning models excel in capturing complex asset return interactions that linear models fail to detect. These relationships or patterns are too complex or hidden for traditional linear models to detect. The patterns avoid detection by linear methods, but ML models can find them. Similarly, (Gu et al., 2020) find that ensemble techniques consistently outperform standard econometric benchmarks in asset pricing tasks. Corroborating evidence, (Bianchi et al., 2021), in another asset class, finds that extreme trees and neural networks deliver stronger bond‑return predictability and larger economic gains than traditional yield‑curve models.
Unsupervised learning techniques refer to machine learning methods that identify patterns or structures in data without pre-labelled outcomes; clustering algorithms such as k-means and hierarchical clustering group similar data points to uncover hidden relationships in financial datasets. in uncovering hidden structures in financial data. These tools are widely used to segment investors, classify firms, and detect behavioral patterns. In addition, core ML concepts such as the bias-variance trade off, feature engineering, and model stability are vital in ensuring that models generalize effectively beyond the training data. However, the obscurity of many ML algorithms presents challenges in regulated domains where understanding model behavior is just as important as performance.
[Heading2] 2.4 Inter-disciplinary Theories
Involving two or more subjects or areas of knowledge, lenses, within AI-driven finance can be beneficial, and using ML models is particularly well-suited to uncover anomalies by identifying patterns that deviate from normative assumptions. This section shows how ML can be beneficial in Behavioral Finance, Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG), and in Information Systems.
Behavioral Finance
Behavioral finance is one such lens, emphasizing that financial decisions are influenced by cognitive biases and emotional factors rather than strict rationality. Our first example, (Jiang et al., 2023), shows how market anomalies continue even in the presence of AI-driven strategies, suggesting that human irrationality remains a significant driver of financial dynamics.
[Heading2] Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG)
ESG frameworks are also central to the literature. These frameworks claim that non-financial factors are material to long-term value creation and risk management. AI models are increasingly used to assess ESG exposures, process unstructured data (financial statements, annual reports, quarterly reports, and proxy statements), and forecast climate-related risks. (Sautner et al., 2023) show how ML techniques can be used to quantify firm-level climate risk exposure and integrate it into financial models, leading to operationalizing ESG theory in a data-rich environment and hence being able to make better decisions.
Information Systems
The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), developed by Fred Davis in 1989, is instrumental in understanding how finance professionals and companies work with AI tools. According to TAM, user acceptance is driven by perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and trust in technology. (Cao et al., 2024) examine this relationship in the context of equity analysis, finding that the effectiveness of human-AI collaboration depends not just on the quality of insights but also on users' confidence in the underlying models.
The Evolution of AI and ML in Financial Research Over Time
The past twenty years have witnessed one of the biggest changes in finance: the use of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML). What began as statistical models evolved into data-driven predictive algorithms, automated platforms, and decision-making frameworks. The literature used in this Literature Review illustrates the change through several themes and time periods. The following are five factors describing the growth of AI and ML in finance:
1.The use of early statistical methods
2.The rise of machine learning models
3.Using Deep Learning in asset pricing
4.Improvements in explainable AI
5.The addition of robo-advisory platforms
[Heading2] 3.1 The First Use of Statistical Methods
Before machine learning became popular, financial analysis was based on traditional statistical models. These included linear regressions, logistic models, and time series frameworks like Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average - ARIMA (a statistical time series forecasting model that combines autoregression, differencing, and moving averages to analyze and predict future values based on past data) and Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity-GARCH: a statistical model used to forecast and analyze time series data with changing volatility over time, particularly in financial markets, which were commonly used to figure out expected returns, measure volatility, and model financial risk. These models put a lot of weight on being able to understand them theoretically, based on the ideas of linearity, normality, and low dimensionality.
These tools were important, but they often had limited use because they could not handle nonlinear interactions or high-dimensional input that are common in modern financial datasets. Nonetheless, this time was important for building the foundation of empirical finance, especially for proving important ideas like market efficiency and factor models.
(Dong et al., 2022) used some traditional approaches to validating models and making inferences. The author explored market anomalies and evaluated machine learning performance using methods like Out-of-Sample Forecasting. 
 3.2 The Rise of Machine Learning (ML) Models
Traditional statistical methods were previously mostly employed in the banking industry, where analysts would utilize regression models (e.g., "Does X really cause Y?") to construct models based on predefined assumptions to ascertain if correlations or causality exist. 
Machine learning, on the other hand, focuses on identifying patterns in large data with the aim of making the best decisions possible based on those patterns, even if we do not begin with a particular theory or presumption. Finance professionals are now using algorithms that learn from data to identify hidden trends and improve predictions, rather than just confirming or refuting hypotheses. This is certainly a major shift in the use of tools, and a change in thinking in decision-making.
Support vector machines (SVMs), random forests, and gradient boosting methods are examples of supervised learning models that have surpassed classical models in tasks such as predicting stock returns, earnings, and credit scores. (Cakici et al., 2024) and (Leippold et al., 2022)compares a set of ML models across a large dataset of stock-level predictors in a systematic way. Their results show that ML models not only make better predictions, while the paper does not explicitly analyze interaction effects or non-additive relationships, its findings suggest that the predictive relationships are complex and contingent on specific factors and methodological implementations, which linear models miss.
(Avramov et al., 2023) also show that multi-factor models based on machine learning are better at adapting to changing relationships in financial markets. These models mark a turning point in methodology, where economic theory is combined with real-world data to guide the model structure instead of forcing it to be that way from the start. In addition, and during this time, unsupervised learning methods (k-means clustering, hierarchical clustering, and dimensionality reduction techniques like PCA) also started to show up and be popular. Practical uses for these methods were market segmentation, peer-group analysis, and feature selection.
[Heading2] 3.3 Using Deep Learning and Neural Networks in Asset Prices
The success of deep learning contributed to making ML models use in finance even more complex.
 Deep neural networks (DNNs), such as multilayer perceptrons, convolutional neural networks (CNNs), and long short-term memory networks (LSTMs), have become more common in the literature, especially in fields where financial signals are not always clear, nonlinear, and time dependent.
(Chen et al., 2024) work focused on using deep learning architectures to find hidden patterns in return predictions. Their work demonstrates that DNNs can abstract information from noisy inputs and enhance signal extraction in high-dimensional settings, a capability that linear models or shallow learners often lack. Another paper, (Jiang et al., 2023),used convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to analyze asset prices by converting historical market data into images and extracting predictive patterns. ​ CNNs identified complex features automatically, enabling accurate predictions that outperformed traditional methods like momentum and reversal. ​ The approach was robust across geographies and time scales, with successful transfer learning to international markets and lower-frequency data, highlighting the power of deep learning in uncovering profitable insights in asset pricing.
Researchers can also use deep learning to model hierarchical relationships, add unstructured inputs like text or images, and create end-to-end models that process data without having to manually create features. Although the models work, people had challenges in understanding them and the need for new tools and frameworks to be interpreted and validated. This is what leads to the next stage of development.
[Heading2] 3.4 Explainable AI (XAI)
One of the main challenges with models is the issue associated with being black boxes. These models often provide high-performance results but lack transparency and interpretability. This creates issues in understanding how these models work, and to justify their decisions to users and stakeholders, such as banks and other financial institutions. Professionals in highly regulated fields, such as banking, asset management, and insurance, cannot rely solely on black-box predictions. Instead, they need to know why a model makes a recommendation to meet standards for compliance, risk management, and ethics.
This need led to the creation of the field of Explainable AI (XAI). (De Bock et al., 2023) stress the need for interpretable AI systems in operational finance and introduce tools like:
A - SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP values), which are a way to explain how each feature (input variable) in a machine learning model contributes to a specific prediction
B - Local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME)
C - Surrogate tree models.
D- Sensitivity Analysis Methods
These tools break down complicated ML outputs into understandable parts that make sense to people.
As technicality is crucial in these models, XAI is also important for building trust, fixing models, and analyzing scenarios. In this way, it is not just a technical addition; it is also a framework for governance and transparency that makes AI systems more accountable when making financial decisions.
[Heading2] 3.5 Robo-Advisory Platforms and Automation
Robo-advisory is an excellent example of how AI evolved in Finance. These Rob-advisory services deliver automated financial advice, asset rebalancing based on the investor's risk profile, and portfolio management. Further development in Robo-advisory has allowed them to expand to institutional asset management, retirement planning, and wealth management. 
As early robo-advisors followed rule-based decision logic, newer generations incorporated machine learning algorithms for real-time analysis, risk assessment, and behavioral profiling. (De Bock et al., 2023) explore how user engagement and trust in AI-driven interfaces are shaped by explainability and perceived usefulness, linking the technological functionality with the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM).
Another significant paper in the robo-advisory space, (D’Acunto et al., 2019, paper 41), evaluates the impact of a robo-advising portfolio optimizer on individual investors, revealing that it significantly improves portfolio diversification and reduces volatility for under diversified investors, leading to better market-adjusted returns. While diversified investors saw minor change in performance, they exhibited increased trading activity and fees without proportional benefits. ​ The tool also reduced behavioral biases like the disposition effect and trend chasing, though not entirely. ​ All users showed heightened portfolio attention post-adoption. ​ The study highlights the potential of robo-advising to benefit under diversified investors while emphasizing the need for tailored interventions to address the varying needs of different investor profiles.
Additionally, (Cardillo & Chiappini, 2024, paper 42), analyses research on robo-advisors (2017–2022), focusing on classification, behavioral aspects, performance, and algorithms. ​ Robo-advisors use AI to provide cost-effective, automated financial advice, benefiting under-diversified and low-income investors, though their performance against market benchmarks is neutral. ​ Key future research areas include regulatory impacts, sustainable investing, financial inclusion, and socioeconomic effects. ​ The study offers insights for scholars and financial institutions, emphasizing the need for deeper exploration of regulatory, behavioral, and algorithmic dimensions. ​
Also, (Acunto & Rossi, 2020, paper 43) examined robo-advising, an automated financial advice system addressing the limitations of traditional advisers, such as biases, high costs, and inaccessibility. ​ Robo-advisors offer low-cost, tailored advice with varying levels of personalization, investor involvement, and human interaction, improving investment decisions and portfolio diversification. ​ While they democratize financial advice, concerns about systemic risks, data privacy, inequality, and trust remain. ​ Hybrid models combining human and algorithmic advice are suggested to increase adoption, especially among older users. ​ The document highlights open questions on holistic advice, algorithmic trust, and regulatory challenges. ​
It is important to note that robo-advisors increasingly rely on machine learning to customize portfolio recommendations and execute investment strategies. While this certainly improves efficiency, the sheer dependence on data-driven models negatively affects investors' trust if users do not understand the reasoning behind the recommendations. (Rossi & Utkus, 2024) discuss how automated investment strategies adapt to changing market trends by continuously learning from new data, thus moving from static portfolio allocation to adaptive, real-time decision systems. Furthermore, argue that robo-advisory should use machine learning not only to optimize predictions, but to deliver recommendations that are explainable, theoretically sound, and robust, leading to maintaining investors' confidence. 
It also pays to mention that robo-advisory signifies a turning point in AI's role. That is, not only used as a decision support tool but as an autonomous agent in the financial ecosystem. This created another level of challenges around oversight, responsibility in machine-related finance.

AI/ML uses in critical areas of finance
Machine learning (ML) in finance has evolved from a theoretical concept to a necessity in real-world applications. Its usefulness is evident in many vital areas where traditional models have problems because they are too complicated, too big, or have a data structure that does not work. This section looks at how ML can be used in four primary areas of finance: predicting returns and pricing assets, reporting and disclosing financial information, analyzing ESG and climate risks, and finding market inefficiencies and anomalies. In each subsection, the authors compile findings from several key studies reviewed in the forty papers.
[Heading2] 4.1 Asset Pricing and Return Prediction
Asset pricing has traditionally relied on linear factor models such as the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) and the Fama-French multi-factor frameworks. While foundational, these models often assume static relationships and linear effects, which limit their ability to capture the full complexity of financial markets. Machine learning offers a powerful alternative by introducing flexibility in functional form, accommodating large sets of predictors, and uncovering latent interactions that may evolve.
One of the most essential advantages that ML provides for this field is its ability to capture nonlinear relationships between asset returns that change over time. For instance, ML-based models can incorporate hundreds of macroeconomic and firm-level input variables without encountering problems with multicollinearity (when two or more independent variables in a regression model are highly correlated, making it difficult to isolate their individual effects on the dependent variable) or overfitting (when a model learns the training data too well, capturing noise and random patterns that reduce its ability to generalize to new, unseen data), which are common issues with traditional regression methods. Ensemble methods like random forests and gradient boosting can dynamically weigh the importance of predictors, showing which signals are most useful under different market conditions.
The complexity of the model is another important aspect. Models with more complex algorithms, when thoroughly evaluated, have a greater ability to generalize to new data. That was evident in (Gu et al., 2020) when they investigated whether more complex models could outperform simpler benchmarks in predicting asset returns. They discover that methods like elastic net (a regularization technique in regression) and random forests (a class of machine learning methods that builds multiple decision trees on random subsets of data and features, then combines their predictions to improve accuracy and reduce overfitting), not only lower error rates but also reveal significant patterns in macro-financial data.
The predictive advantage of ML is also present in fixed income markets, especially in using machine learning to predict bond risk premiums. (Bianchi et al., 2021) demonstrate that ensemble models outperform conventional regression-based approaches in capturing hidden macroeconomic and financial risk components. Their findings show that ML approaches are better at dealing with noisy, macro-sensitive data.
Finally, venture capital joined the fields, enjoying the benefits of using ML in its ability to combine behavioral and contextual data. (Bubna et al., 2020) demonstrates the advantage of using machine learning (ML) by employing unsupervised techniques to classify venture capital (VC) communities into distinct clusters to identify key drivers of these clusters, such as VC age, centrality, and portfolio firm size. ML achieves a high classification accuracy (93.9%) and uncovers patterns of homogeneity within communities and differences between clusters, providing insights into the dynamics of VC syndication. ​ These techniques enable the paper to analyze complex datasets and reveal the economic impact of VC communities on innovation and firm maturation, insights that would be difficult to achieve with traditional methods.
To put it briefly, machine learning (ML) not only facilitates the estimation of returns across asset classes but also uncovers novel patterns in financial data that challenge conventional models and supplement the empirical data that forms the basis of asset pricing research.
4.2 Disclosure and Financial ReportingFinancial disclosure, encompassing investor presentations, earnings reports, and regulatory filings, is a critical component of market transparency and price discovery. Historically, sentiment analysis based on dictionaries and models that utilize keyword frequency has been employed to analyze these disclosures. While beneficial, these methods often failed to capture the context or nuances of financial language adequately. Natural language processing (NLP) and machine learning have transformed this domain by enabling the automatic interpretation of narrative text in more complex and predictive ways.Machine learning models are capable of identifying sentiment shifts, extracting hidden variables that influence investor behavior, and learning deeper connections among words and phrases, especially when trained on labelled financial text. These methods enhance our ability to quantify information asymmetry, forecast market reactions, and identify the tone of managerial decisions. (Frankel et al., 2022) conduct a comprehensive comparison of ML-based sentiment models and traditional dictionary methods utilizing corporate disclosures. Research indicates that machine learning techniques are more effective at identifying predictive signals in earnings reports and management discussions. The machine learning models demonstrate superior performance compared to dictionary-based models regarding correlations with future returns and analyst revisions. This indicates that machine learning improves the practical utility of forecasting alongside its interpretive precision.Machine learning (ML) enables the modeling of cross-sectional return patterns and post-earnings announcement drift (PEAD) by utilizing firm characteristics, in addition to sentiment analysis. The paper "Cross-Sectional Expected Returns" by (Han et al., 2024) employs supervised learning algorithms to predict future returns utilizing various accounting, disclosure, and valuation factors. The research demonstrates that machine learning outperforms linear models by dynamically weighting predictors and capturing nonlinear relationships.The studies demonstrate a clear evolution from static, keyword-based financial text analysis to advanced, context-sensitive predictive modeling. Machine learning is poised to play a critical role in extracting actionable insights from financial narratives as the complexity and volume of disclosure requirements escalate, especially within regulatory frameworks such as IFRS and ESG mandates.
[Heading2] 4.3 ESG and Climate Risk
In recent finance research, driven by regulatory requirements, investor preferences, and a stronger awareness of climate-related risks, Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) challenges have gained significant importance. What is a crucial point of mention here is that ESG and climate data are often heterogeneous, unstructured, and difficult to quantify using conventional econometric models. This presents a needed opportunity for machine learning (ML), which excels at managing noisy and high-dimensional inputs and uncovering patterns that are otherwise hidden in nonstandard formats.
One major challenge lies in assessing a firm's exposure to climate change based on disclosures, news coverage, and market behavior. ML methods, especially natural language processing and clustering algorithms, are increasingly used to translate qualitative climate information into quantifiable signals. These signals, in turn, contribute to risk-adjusted valuations, sovereign credit assessment, and ESG scoring in general.
(Sautner et al., 2023) using textual analysis of earnings calls and investor responses developed a machine-learning-based measure of firm-level climate change exposure. Their work reveals that firms with higher exposure receive more investor scrutiny and face higher risk premiums. This demonstrates that textual data, when processed with ML, can yield material risk insights.
In equity markets, (Ardia et al., 2023) compare green vs. brown stock performance and use machine‑learning topic modeling to parse climate‑risk news, which helps isolate ESG‑driven pricing effects beyond sector and market influences. Their results show that data‑driven (ML) approaches more cleanly identify the return impact of climate concerns than traditional risk models, implying those models may understate the financial materiality of ESG.
On the sovereign level, (Klusak et al., 2023) use ML to project climate-adjusted sovereign credit ratings under multiple temperature rise scenarios. Their model integrates macroeconomic, environmental, and governance variables to simulate how countries' creditworthiness may develop due to climate stress. This approach opens a window through which ML can inform not just asset pricing but also long-term systemic risk management.
[Heading2] 4.4 Market Efficiency and Anomalies
Decades of empirical research reveal a recurring pricing anomaly that runs counter to the EMH theory. An example of these anomalies is momentum effects, value-growth spreads, earnings surprises, and post-announcement drifts. All along, while traditional explanations of these anomalies point to behavioral biases or omitted risk factors, the advent of machine learning (ML) has provided new tools to re-examine these market irregularities with greater statistical precision and model flexibility.
One key advantage of ML is its ability to analyze large datasets across multiple times structures and economic environments, Model misspecification (models built with incorrect assumptions, structure, or variables) or overfitting are two problems with statistical errors that arise while utilizing statistical models. Using ML can help differentiate between true market efficiencies and those statistical errors. 
For example, Dong et al. (2021, Paper 16) examines how long-short anomaly portfolio returns can predict market excess returns using machine learning methods. ​ It finds that these anomalies provide strong and reliable signals for forecasting.​ By using machine learning to avoid overfitting, the study shows that anomalies are valuable tools for predicting market returns. 
The empirical validity of anomalies also varies across markets and investor behavior. In this context, (Leippold et al., 2022) examine market efficiency in the Chinese stock market using a range of machine learning models. One main finding is that ML improves predictive accuracy significantly over traditional models, especially in segments dominated by retail investors and state-owned enterprises. They find that neural networks outperform other ML approaches in forecasting returns in less efficient markets, raising questions about the acceptance of EMH.
Furthermore, (Kaniel et al., 2023) apply ML to measure the ability of mutual fund managers in selecting profitable securities. By modeling alpha generation through hierarchical models and performance attribution frameworks, they reveal that managers consistently outperform benchmarks, even after adjusting for risk and factor exposures. These findings revive the debate over whether alpha (the excess return of an investment relative to the return of a benchmark index or market standard, after adjusting for risk and factor exposures) is merely due to “luck” or indicative of genuine informational advantages.
Finally, the role of explainable AI in understanding anomaly-based trading strategies was the topic of (Leung et al., 2021). Their work emphasizes that ML-based strategies must be transparent and interpretable, particularly in regulatory contexts where black-box models are discouraged. This signifies the idea that market anomalies, if persistent, require clear theoretical and operational framing, not just statistical discovery.
Taken together, these papers highlight a nuanced view: machine learning neither confirms nor refutes market efficiency outright. Instead, it redefines the analytical boundaries of how we identify, evaluate, and interpret anomalies in the presence of complex, dynamic data environments.
Consequences for ethics, rules, and governance
The growing use of machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) in financial systems raises not only technical and predictive issues but also major ethical, regulatory, and governance challenges. Machine Learning algorithms are not easy to understand, known as the black box problem, compared to traditional financial models which use linear regression or logistic regression that are built on a foundation of established economic and financial theories. This complexity presents challenges in accountability, fairness, and supervision, particularly when these systems are implemented in high-risk domains such as credit lending, asset pricing, algorithmic trading, and consumer finance. 
As regulatory organizations start to catch up with how quickly technology is being adopted, banks and other financial institutions need to answer certain important questions: 
1 -Can we explain and defend automated decisions?
2 -How about stopping bias or discrimination by algorithms from happening?
3 -And what frameworks make sure these systems act properly in a market where there is a lot of competition? 
The section talks about these challenges in three primary areas: explainability and openness, algorithmic ethics and collusion, and fairness and disclosure duties. 
[Heading2] 5.1 Bias, Openness, and Clarity 
As a starting point, we recognize that one of the primary challenges in applying ML to inform financial decisions is that the logic underlying the models is often unclear. Traditional linear regression models have coefficients that are easy to understand, but machine learning systems, especially neural networks, and ensemble models, do not enjoy these characteristics. This creates a lack of clarity, which makes stakeholders untrusting and less willing to follow current rules that require explanations of results. 
The paper by (De Bock et al., 2023) highlight the significance of Explainable AI (XAI) in mitigating this issue. Their study supports the use of technologies that make things easier to understand, including SHAP values (which measure the impact of each feature by quantifying how much it has pushed the model's prediction away from a baseline) and surrogate modeling (a simplified, data-driven approximation of a more complex, high-fidelity model), in operational financial systems. These technologies help people understand ML output better and help make regulations clearer. Examples of these advantages are evident in loan approvals, credit scores, and finding financial fraud.
[Heading2] 5.2 Collusive Conduct and Algorithmic Morality
In addition to problems with interpretability, ML might cause behaviors to arise that are morally and legally questionable. In competitive settings, algorithms designed to enhance performance may inadvertently acquire behaviors that resemble collusive techniques, even in the absence of clear coordination or human supervision. 
Abada & Lambin, 2023 examine this within the framework of autonomous pricing agents, illustrating that rational, profit-maximizing algorithms can align with market strategies that diminish price competition. Their results call into question traditional legal definitions of collusion that are dependent on communication and purpose. Authorities determining accountability for outcomes resulting from system design versus intentional misconduct becomes more challenging as AI gains autonomy. This potential issue calls on organizations to research and investigate the use of ethical design principles and risk testing based on simulation in the AI governance space.
[Heading2] 5.3 Governance and Disclosure
As machine learning is used more in financial services for consumers, its ability to make economic inequality worse has gotten a lot of attention. ML systems can pick up patterns that are related to race, gender, and income, which can lead to different effects that traditional governance frameworks cannot fix.
Fuster et al., 2022 investigate the application of ML models in mortgage underwriting. These findings indicate that although these models enhance overall risk prediction, they simultaneously lead to increased pricing differences within racial groups. Minority applicants must deal with higher and more unpredictable interest rates, even when creditworthiness is considered. Algorithmic fairness audits are crucial in this case, and it is essential to have disclosure rules that extend beyond input attributes to encompass outcomes and model behavior. 
The results show that old ways of making sure people follow the rules, like leaving out protected variables (the practice of deliberately excluding sensitive attributes such as race, gender, religion, national origin, age, and disability from the training data), aren't working anymore in this age of algorithmic reasoning.

[Heading2] 6.1 Machine Learning Methodologies
Using ML in Finance in recent years has become the methodology of choice for multi-dimensional financial datasets covering various areas within Finance (see Figure 6.1a), such as asset pricing, risk modeling, and sentiment analysis. It is appealing because it is flexible and learns directly from data, rather than being limited by strict assumptions. 
The literature also reveals a dominant reliance on supervised learning, particularly in forecasting and classification tasks (Figure 6.1b).
Random forests, gradient boosting machines, and deep neural networks are among the most common techniques used in this field. They are particularly effective at predicting returns and estimating risk because they can proficiently manage large sets of correlated inputs and uncover nonlinear relationships. For example, Gu et al. (2020) benchmarked a wide variety of supervised models against one another. The results showed that deep learning and boosting methods offer significantly better predictive accuracy than standard linear models, a finding that holds true for both in-sample and out-of-sample tests. This underscores how machine learning can extract valuable return signals that are often overlooked by conventional approaches.
Deep learning approaches, particularly feedforward neural networks, have further expanded the methodological frontier. In (Chen et al., 2024), A deep learning model was developed for asset pricing that identifies nonlinear patterns in company data. This allowed it to capture complex interactions and maintain strong performance through changing market conditions, marking a significant improvement over traditional factor-based model. At the same time, Natural Language Processing (NLP) is now being used to extract insights from unstructured financial texts like earnings calls and analyst reports. Frankel et al., 2022, used advanced models for sentiment analysis, finding that they far surpassed traditional dictionary methods in both nuance and predictive accuracy. Understanding the context and tone of text brings a vital new dimension to financial modeling, particularly for studying investor behavior. 
The main drawback of the above models is their 'black box' nature. To solve this, Explainable AI (XAI) techniques like SHAP and LIME are now used to interpret and clarify their predictions. In (De Bock et al., 2023), they emphasized the value of these interpretability tools in operational research, especially for regulated environments where decision accountability is crucial.
[Heading2] 6.2 Traditional Econometric Approaches
Even with the growth of machine learning, traditional econometric models are still essential tools in finance because they are easy to interpret, straightforward, and based on established economic theories. These include:
Linear and logistic regression models, commonly used in credit scoring and financial ratio analysis.
Panel regressions, which are applied to longitudinal firm-level data.
ARIMA and GARCH models for time-series analysis of volatility and returns.
Fama-MacBeth regressions, foundational in testing asset pricing anomalies.
These models are the right tool when your main goal is to assess an idea and explain why something happens, not just to get the most accurate forecast. Their biggest weakness, however, is that they cannot manage the complicated patterns found in today's massive and complex data.
To solve this problem, researchers are adding economic rules to machine learning models to make them "smarter." A 2022 study by (Avramov et al., 2023), tested this and found mixed results. Forcing the models to follow economic logic made them easier to understand, but it also often made them less accurate at making predictions than the standard, more flexible ML models.
The use of econometric benchmarking is also prominent in anomaly research. Gu et al., 2020, applied standard regression-based anomaly tests to contrast the effectiveness of ML-based anomaly detectors. Their research reveals that machine learning can find lasting patterns in the market that simpler, traditional models overlook. This happens most often when different market forces combine and influence each other in complicated ways.
A hybrid trend is also emerging, combining ML’s power to uncover hidden variables with the structure of econometric models. Rather than viewing traditional statistics and machine learning as competing approaches, the most effective strategy is to use them as partners. Machine learning excels at discovering new patterns in complex data, which are then validated for robustness and reliability using established statistical methods. This has been discussed in the work done by:
Gu et al., 2020: focused on comparing machine learning methods with traditional approaches for predicting asset returns and measuring risk premiums. ​ It emphasized the ability of machine learning methods, such as neural networks and regression trees, to capture complex nonlinear interactions and improve predictive accuracy.
Avramov et al., 2023: imposed economic restrictions on machine learning models (excluding microcaps, nonrated firms, and distressed firms), balancing predictive power with interpretability. 
Han et al., 2024: employed Fama-MacBeth regressions to evaluate signals generated by machine learning, illustrating the complementarity between machine learning and econometrics.
Leung et al., 2021: emphasized that while ML is powerful for return prediction, econometric methods remain crucial for ensuring statistical credibility.
Hansen & Siggaard, 2024: Argues for merging hypothesis‑driven finance with modern ML to enable valid high‑dimensional inference. Using DML, they let ML model the complex “nuisance” part while delivering an interpretable, econometric coefficient of interest, showing how ML discovery and econometric inference can work together.
(Chen et al., 2024): Builds a theory‑guided (no‑arbitrage) stochastic discount factor with deep learning, an explicit example of embedding economic structure into ML so the model adapts to data while remaining consistent with pricing theory.
[Heading2] 6.3 Benchmarking and Performance Evaluation
The effectiveness of any model hinges on its validation and evaluation, not solely on its construction. In the reviewed literature, out-of-sample accuracy is consistently the preferred quantitative metric for assessing performance.
Across the reviewed literature, scholars emphasize that the effectiveness of any predictive model hinges on rigorous validation rather than construction alone, with out-of-sample accuracy consistently identified as the decisive metric of performance.
For instance, (Gu et al., 2020), highlight that in-sample fit can be misleading without robustness checks, stressing the primacy of held-out testing (a model validation technique where a portion of the available data is "held out" (set aside) during the training process and used exclusively for testing the model's performance). Similarly, (Gu et al., 2020) and (Kelly et al., 2024) underline that the predictive credibility of machine learning models arises only when they demonstrate stable out-of-sample performance. emphasize that model validity rests on forecasting exercises beyond the training sample, as in-sample fit alone can be misleading. 
This insistence on external validation extends to more specialized applications: (Sautner et al., 2023) emphasize out-of-sample tests when applying textual and ML approaches to climate risk. Likewise, (Frankel et al., 2022) and (Bianchi et al., 2021) both demonstrate that hybrid econometric ML models succeed only when validated on unseen (out-of-sample) data, underscoring that predictive credibility rests on performance beyond the training sample. In a similar manner, (Hansen & Siggaard, 2024) underscore that out-of-sample testing is the essential safeguard against spurious results in return prediction.
For regression tasks, metrics such as mean squared error (MSE), R², and Sharpe ratios are most employed. For classification tasks, particularly in fraud detection and credit default prediction, ROC-AUC, precision–recall curves, and F1-scores are standard. (Cakici et al., 2024) and (Chen et al., 2024) both emphasize the importance of robust out-of-sample validation using rolling and expanding windows, which simulate real-world predictive scenarios.
The stability of the model is also rigorously tested with cross-validation protocols, including k-fold (a technique that systematically tests a model's performance by splitting data into k equal parts and rotating which part serves as the test set) and rolling time-series windows (also called walk-forward validation, is a cross-validation technique designed specifically for time series data that maintains chronological order, crucial in finance where you can't use future information to predict the past). This step is crucial for confirming that the model's performance is not artificially inflated by overfitting or idiosyncrasies in the training data.
Citing a need for more comprehensive evaluation, researchers like (De Bock et al., 2023) advocate for performance benchmarks that extend beyond statistical accuracy to include interpretability, stakeholder impact, and regulatory alignment. This reflects a broader shift in evaluation protocols, where ethical considerations such as fairness, explainability, and auditability are becoming paramount in the comparison of financial models.
(Frankel et al., 2022) assesses sentiment models by comparing their explanatory power for stock returns, such as adjusted R², and their ability to predict future financial outcomes, including earnings surprises and hedge portfolio returns. ​ This approach highlights the economic significance of sentiment measures, grounding technical evaluations in concrete financial realities. Their findings demonstrate that machine-learning methods, particularly the random forest regression tree model, outperform dictionary-based methods in capturing disclosure sentiment and its impact on post-disclosure stock returns. ​
Gaps in the Literature on Machine Learning and Modern Finance: A Narrative Synthesis of Themes and Challenges
Machine learning has reshaped finance, but there is still a gap between what appears in journals and what holds up in finance as a practice. Yes, we have seen real progress, neural nets that forecast returns, AI tools that rebalance portfolios, but most studies gloss over the gritty parts of live markets. Transaction costs and slippage erode signals, models that shine in back tests break in production, and opaque algorithms are hard to defend to risk teams and regulators.
This section can be used to set out multiple research agendas: ten concrete challenges/problems grouped under five broad themes. Think of the themes as the meta‑gaps; the numbered items are the actionable sub‑gaps. Each theme marks a recurring weakness in the literature, and each sub‑gap translates that weakness into specific, testable work (methods to try, data to assemble, evaluations to run).
Because these issues are linked in practice, some items naturally serve more than one theme. For example, measurement and evaluation standards sit at the core of both implementability and interpretability; you cannot judge a model’s real value without cost‑aware, robust, and explainable benchmarks.
In short, looking across these forty papers, the five themes give the scaffolding; the ten paragraphs are the bricks. Together, they move from high-level diagnosis (what’s missing) to concrete, doable studies (how to fill it) without losing sight of costs, causality, portability, market plumbing, and the human role.
[Heading2] Themes
Models look powerful in-sample, but frictions and constraints erode their edge.
Complexity helps prediction, yet interpretability and causal grounding lag.
External validity across markets is uneven.
The microstructure layer can undo model assumptions.
Human–machine collaboration is still under-theorized.
[Heading2] Challenges
[Heading2] 1) From statistical power to implementable edge.
Deep, flexible models often beat linear baselines on paper, but the edge shrinks fast once you layer in commissions, bid–ask, market impact, turnover, and capacity limits. Results attenuate sharply when you drop microcaps or distressed names, and when you price in limits-to-arbitrage, the very settings where many backtests look best (Avramov et al., 2023). You see the same tension in fixed income: nonlinear models forecast bond risk premia, but the channel varies by maturity and leans on macro series that are costly to time in real portfolios (Bianchi et al., 2021).
Gap: design cost‑aware, capacity‑aware learners that maximize investor utility under realistic frictions, not just predictive fit.
[Heading2] 2) Complexity helps, but theory still matters!
There is now a theory behind the “virtue of complexity”: with proper regularization, high‑parameter models can outperform simpler ones (Kelly et al., 2024). Empirically, trees and neural nets earn their keep by capturing interactions that linear models miss (Gu et al., 2020). Yet desks, boards, and regulators need explanations and stable effect sizes. Recent tools, double‑ML and regularized Fama–MacBeth, recover interpretable payoffs and valid inference in high‑dimensional settings (Hansen & Siggaard, 2024) and (Han et al., 2024). 
Gap: We need standard workflows that combine powerful predictions with clear explanations of what drives returns, along with upfront testing plans, so quants can effectively communicate their findings to the executives who make investment decisions.
[Heading2] 3) Robustness and external validity
Some signals vanish when you leave the original lab conditions: change the anomaly list, the portfolio rules, or the evaluation window, and predictability can disappear, especially outside the US (Cakici et al., 2024). Market design matters too: in China, liquidity, retail dominance, and short‑sale frictions shift which signals work and how (Leippold et al., 2022). And with thousands of plausible predictors, in‑sample tests overstate power even if markets are efficient; out‑of‑sample designs keep their economic meaning (Martin & Nagel, 2022).
Gap: We need methods for making models work across different markets, where researchers commit upfront to their assumptions about time periods, trading costs, and position limits, and report these real-world constraints alongside their performance numbers.
[Heading2] 4) Trends, technical, and regime risk
Modern ML has reopened the technical‑analysis debate. Image‑ and sequence‑based models extract price‑pattern information that predicts the cross‑section and can deliver alpha at times (Murray et al., 2024). However, profits often fade as markets learn, and drawdowns tend to cluster around regime shifts.
Gap: We need clear rules for managing trading models, when to update them, how much they can lose, and how big positions can get, so they do not accidentally turn into dangerous bets on rare market crashes.
[Heading2] 5) Risk models and hedging
ML improves beta forecasts and reduces hedging errors; its nonlinearity avoids persistent tail under‑ or over‑estimation and supports stronger market‑neutral construction (Drobetz et al., 2024). What is thin is the handoff to portfolio construction and stress testing.
Gap: We need complete systems that connect ML risk predictions directly to portfolio construction, stress testing, and the actual cash required to hold positions, not just isolated risk models that do not talk to the rest of the trading system.
[Heading2] 6) Climate, contracts, and text
Climate risk, at the firm level and in sovereign credit, (Klusak et al., 2023). Text unlocks deeper structure: you can measure firm complexity and mine securitization contracts to predict performance and agency frictions (Loughran & McDonald, 2024) and (Ambrose et al., 2024).
Gap: We need to incorporate climate risks and contract language into pricing models to understand the actual mechanisms, how climate events affect company cash flows, borrowing costs, and loan terms, rather than just finding correlations in the data.
[Heading2] 7) Microstructure, fairness, and real-time frictions
Municipal bond pricing shows strategic behavior; HFT in equities spills into options liquidity, (Griffin et al., 2023). Credit ML can raise efficiency without worsening equity if you handle selective labels and impose fairness constraints (Tantri, 2021).
Gap: We need ML systems that balance making good predictions, executing trades efficiently, and following regulations, all while understanding how actions in one market affect related markets.
[Heading2] 8) Humans plus machines
Pure‑AI models beat many analysts on transparent, data‑rich tasks, but humans add value on institutional nuance, intangibles, distress, ambiguous disclosures, and hybrid workflows cut extreme errors (Cao et al., 2024). Robo‑advice tells a similar story at scale: better diversification, fewer biases, more indexing, and improved risk‑adjusted outcomes (Rossi & Utkus, 2024) and (D’Acunto et al., 2019).
Gap: We need to figure out when humans should override algorithms, what information they need to make good decisions, and how to incentivize human-machine teamwork, then test whether this collaboration holds up during market crashes.
[Heading2] 9) Dynamics and control
New AI techniques can now solve complex financial problems with thousands of moving parts that change continuously over time. This breakthrough lets us build more realistic models of how companies make investment decisions, how they choose between debt and equity, and how assets get priced in real markets (Duarte et al., 2024).
Gap: We need systems that seamlessly connect ML predictions (what to buy/sell) with real-time trading decisions (when to trade, how to hedge, how to fund positions), all in one integrated workflow instead of separate disconnected steps.
[Heading2] 10) Measurement discipline
Even when ML wins statistically, economic gains depend on turnover, capacity, and implementation (Leung et al., 2021). In text, ML sentiment beats dictionaries, but portability and drift are live issues (Frankel et al., 2022).
Gap: We need agreed-upon ways to test models that everyone can replicate, measuring actual profits after trading costs and size limits, tracking worst losses and consistency over time, and showing how results change depending on which data source you use.
Conclusion
This review synthesized findings from forty core studies across theory, methods, empirical domains, governance, and open problems (Sections 2–7). Three broad conclusions emerge.
First, genuinely predictive gains from ML are real, but only when validated rigorously and interpreted responsibly. Across asset pricing, risk estimation, and text analytics, flexible models (trees, boosted ensembles, deep nets) consistently outperform linear baselines by capturing nonlinearities and interactions that theory-first specifications miss. However, these performance improvements are only credible if validated through rigorous out-of-sample testing; complex models that perform exceptionally well on training data often show diminished returns when tested under real-world conditions.
Second, implementability and external validity are the binding constraints. Once realistic frictions such as transaction costs, capacity limits, and cross-market portability are introduced, profitability often diminishes. This highlights the need for cost-aware, capacity-aware, and institutionally sensitive applications of ML.
Third, new data modalities, text, climate, and contracts, are now material to pricing and risk. ML methods applied to disclosures, climate exposures, and contractual agreements generate risk-relevant state variables absent from traditional factor menus, broadening the empirical toolkit available to finance.
Two tensions persist across the literature: the balance between accuracy, interpretability, and fairness, and the evolving role of human-machine collaboration. While high-performing models are often opaque, explainability and fairness audits remain critical. At the same time, hybrid approaches, where human expertise and machine efficiency complement one another, emerge as particularly promising for reducing errors and bias.
Positioning for the thesis. Through the process of reviewing forty scholarly papers and engaging in sustained dialogue with my supervisor, it has become evident that the findings are not only theoretically significant but also carry substantial practical implications. The collective evidence shows how AI and machine learning are already transforming diverse areas of finance, ranging from asset pricing and trading strategies to risk management, corporate governance, and financial intermediation. What began as an exploration of methodological innovation has opened a broader research avenue: moving beyond AI as a purely predictive or diagnostic tool, toward deploying it in ways that directly enhance financial practice and education.
Accordingly, as I move into the second phase of my DBA, the thesis proposal, I intend to build on this foundation by focusing on a more applied dimension: “Leveraging Artificial Intelligence to Automate the Development of Financial Literacy Content: Opportunities and Limitations.” This research will extend the insights of the literature review into a practice-oriented domain, exploring how AI can be integrated into gamification techniques, eLearning platforms, and simulation-based environments to design scalable, engaging, and effective financial literacy content. This pivot preserves the academic rigor of the review while directing the research toward solutions that are not only defensible in theory but also impactful in practice, with the potential to shape both educational strategies and financial decision-making outcomes.
