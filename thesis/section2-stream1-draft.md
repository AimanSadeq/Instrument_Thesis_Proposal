## 2.1 AI in finance: from technical applications to competence development

The point of departure for this research is the transformation that artificial intelligence has already worked on finance itself. My Year 1 literature review synthesized forty studies from leading finance journals, complemented by a scan of 145 abstracts, and found consistent evidence that machine learning now outperforms traditional econometric baselines across core financial tasks: empirical asset pricing (Gu, Kelly & Xiu, 2020; Kelly, Malamud & Zhou, 2024), risk estimation (Drobetz, Hollstein, Otto & Prokopczuk, 2024), credit allocation (Fuster, Goldsmith-Pinkham, Ramadorai & Walther, 2022), textual analysis of disclosures (Frankel, Jennings & Lee, 2022), and retail investment advice through robo-advisory platforms (D'Acunto & Rossi, 2020; Rossi & Utkus, 2024). The review concluded that these gains are real but conditional — on rigorous out-of-sample validation, on implementability once market frictions are considered, and on governance frameworks capable of managing opacity and bias — and that hybrid arrangements, in which human expertise and machine efficiency complement one another, are among the most promising configurations in the field.

That conclusion opens the question this thesis pursues. Finance increasingly runs on AI, yet the way professionals learn finance has barely changed: lecture-led delivery and static materials remain the norm in corporate and government training. If AI can price assets and screen credit, can it also design the training through which professionals acquire financial competence — and under what conditions is such AI-designed training effective, trusted, and adopted?

The educational-technology literature suggests the question is timely but unanswered. Systematic reviews of AI in education document rapid growth alongside a persistent skew: research overwhelmingly examines AI applied to students in formal higher education, and rarely to instructors, instructional designers, or professional learners (Zawacki-Richter, Marín, Bond & Gouverneur, 2019; Crompton & Burke, 2023). Reviews of large language models in education map dozens of use cases — content generation among the most promising — while noting that evidence from authentic professional settings remains thin (Yan et al., 2024). Early studies of practicing instructional designers working with generative AI find enthusiastic but improvised adoption: practitioners integrating AI across their design workflows without validated principles to guide them (Muljana et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2024). The human–machine collaboration tension identified in the finance literature thus reappears, unresolved, at the point where finance training is produced: generative AI can now draft the materials, but the field lacks evidence on how the AI–human division of design labor affects quality, engagement, and outcomes.

This study stands at that junction, extending the Year 1 inquiry from AI as an analytical instrument within financial markets to AI as a productive capability for developing the financial competence of the professionals who operate them.
